Australian public art collections

From galleries to the streets: where Australian art is held

Public art is encountered in two ways: inside galleries and in the shared spaces of a city. This story begins with the national gallery network, then moves into the distinct collections of Brisbane and Melbourne.

Part 1

The national collection network

Before looking closely at individual artworks, start with the institutions that make art publicly accessible across Australia.

A national network of public galleries

Each dot marks one gallery with a usable location in the available dataset. The pattern follows Australia’s populated east and south-east coasts, establishing the national context for the city collections below.

How to read it: Hover over a dot to see the gallery and its address. Dense groups of dots indicate many nearby public art venues.

Gallery coverage varies by state

The first map shows individual places. This view changes the question: which states and territories have the largest number of mappable galleries?

How to read it: Larger circles mean more galleries with usable coordinates. Circles are placed at capitals for comparison; they do not show the exact location of every gallery.

Part 2

When art becomes part of the city

Gallery maps show where collections can be visited. Public-art records reveal how artworks are distributed across streets, parks and civic precincts.

Art in Brisbane’s public spaces

Brisbane City Council’s collection places artworks throughout the city. Grouping materials makes its physical character visible, from stone and metal works to newer synthetic or light-based pieces.

How to read it: Colour shows the main material group. Click a legend item to highlight it. Where several artworks share one recorded location, the map gently separates their points so each work remains visible.

Melbourne’s collection is led by sculpture

Melbourne’s dataset describes artworks by classification rather than material. Sculpture is the largest recorded group, while memorials, illustrations and other forms occupy a smaller share of the mapped collection.

How to read it: Each point is an artwork and property boundaries provide local context. Click a classification in the legend to focus on it. These categories are Melbourne’s source classifications, so they should not be read as a direct material comparison with Brisbane.

Canberra’s collection mixes sculpture with civic features

In the ACT records, sculpture is the largest source classification, followed by features. Street art, murals and tiled works add a different visual language across Canberra’s suburbs. These are the ACT’s own categories, so they provide a local reading rather than a direct comparison with Brisbane’s materials or Melbourne’s classifications.

How to read it: Each point is one ACT public-art record. Colour shows its source classification; click a legend item to bring that group forward. Hover over a point for its suburb, material, purpose and available commissioning detail.

Three cities, one set of categories

Brisbane, Melbourne and Canberra each describe their public art differently. To compare them, we sorted every source label into five shared groups. Canberra's collection spreads across the whole city, Melbourne's gathers tightly around the centre, and Brisbane's sits somewhere between.

How to read it: Each panel is the same size but zoomed to its own city, so check the scale bar. Hover over a dot to see the shared group and the council's original label. Grey dots are unclassified, not "unimportant". Brisbane's records list only materials (bronze, granite and so on), not artwork types, so most of its dots stay grey. Only a few could be sorted from their titles.

How the groups were made

Sculpture / object: "Sculpture" labels. Mural / wall art: Street Art, Painting Mural, Legal Graffiti Sites. Monument / memorial: "Memorials". Fountain, Functional: assigned only when the title clearly says so (for example "fountain" or "seating"). Other: everything else, including Canberra's "Feature", tiled and vinyl works and Melbourne's "Unknown".
